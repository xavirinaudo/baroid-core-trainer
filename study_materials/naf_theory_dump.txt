Power Law Rheological Model 
 
Governing equation: 
 
 t = K gn 
 
Model parameters: 
–  τ =  shear stress, lb/100ft2 
– K  =  consistency index  
–  γ  = shear rate 
– N   = flow index  
 
 
K Consistency index  
– A term used to determine the “viscosity “effects of a flowing fluid used in the power law and 
Herschel-Bulkley models. It is similar in concept to the PV in the Bingham model but the units 
are not viscosity units in the true sense. 
– The higher “K” value the higher the shear stress and consequently the higher the viscosity. 
 
N Flow index  
– The numerical relation between a fluid’s shear stress and shear rate on a log/log plot. This value 
describes a fluid’s degree of shear-thinning behavior. 
• 
In Newtonian fluids N=1 
• 
In Non-Newtonian fluids N < 1 
 
 
Power Law method tries to overcome the 
Bingham method flaws by providing the low 
shear rate spectrum analysis with an 
exponential profile. 
 
Hydraulic analysis with this method is more 
accurate at low shear rates 
 
This rheological model always will under 
estimate the hydraulic impact on an 
operation, since it assumes the force to 
initiate flow is 0 (which is not realistic).

Herschel and Bulkley Rheological Model 
 
Governing equation: 
 
 t = t0 + K gn 
 
Model parameters: 
–  τ shear stress, lb/100ft2 
–  τ0 yield stress, lb/100ft2 
– K  =  consistency index  
–  γ  = shear rate, sec-1 
– N   = flow index 
 
 
Shear Rate (g) 
– This term is appropriate for laminar flow only. In a flowing fluid, numerically it is the change in 
fluid velocity from one streamline to another divided by the distance between them. 
 
Shear Stress (t) 
– The force per unit area required to shear a fluid at a given shear rate. Shear stress is measured 
on oil field viscometers by the deflection of the FANN 35 meter’s dial at a given shear speed. 
The rheometer dial reading is 
– sometimes incorrectly referred to as shear stress. 
 
Yield Stress (t0) 
– The force required to initiate flow. Yield stress is a time-independent measurement and is 
usually denoted in the Heshchel-Bulkley (yield-power law [YPL]) model as Tau0 and in the 
Bingham model as YP. 
 
The Hershel-Bulkley rheological model fits Bingham plastic fluids, power 
law fluids, and everything else in between. 
 
To calculate is required: 
 
A computer algorithm to obtain solutions.  
 
A minimum of three shear-stress/shear-rate measurements for 
solution.  
 
Model accuracy is improved with additional data input.  
 
We can calculate Tau0 using DFG and WELLSIGHT Express

Important hydraulic terms 
- 
Reynolds number (NRe)  
A dimensionless, numerical term governs whether a flowing fluid will be in laminar or turbulent flow. Often, a 
Reynolds number greater than 2,100 will mark the onset of turbulent flow, but this is not always so.  
 
- 
Critical Reynolds number (NRec) or Critical Velocity 
This value corresponds to the Reynolds number at which laminar flow turns to turbulent flow.  
- 
Critical Flow Rate (Qc)  
Flow rate at which the flow changes from laminar to turbulent.

Pressure Drop (ΔP/ΔL)  
Frictional forces develop when fluids flow through a pipe or an annulus. As a result, fluid energy dissipates. 
These frictional forces are referred to as pressure drops, and are usually referred to as a pressure per unit 
length. The longer a pipe or annulus, the greater is the pressure drop.

Equivalent Circulating Density, ECD is the pressure exerted downhole by the fluid(s), choke pressure,

pressure expressed in terms of a fluid density that is required to get an equivalent pressure at a given depth.

Overview 
Water based drilling fluids were formulated using native clays at the beginning of rotary drilling, and became the 
standard for the industry.  As reservoirs got deeper, new challenges were faced with abnormally pressurized, 
reactive or naturally fractured formations. 
In order to overcome the challenges from WBF, Baroid engineered and release to the industry the first invert 
emulsion fluid in the 1960’s, the INVERMUL drilling system using organophilic clays 
The system provided considerable advantages over WBF, such as: 
– Better hole stability than water fluids 
– More tolerant to contaminants 
– Superior temperature stability 
– Excellent Lubricity 
– Provides corrosion resistance

High-Performance Invert Emulsion Fluids 
– They are free of organophilic clays and also free of organophilic lignites. 
– Their solid content is as low as possible, using Polymer technology for suspension and filtration 
control. 
– The continuous phase is a non-aqueous base fluid called (NAP) such as ester, paraffin, mineral 
oil, diesel or combination of these. 
– High-performance clay-free IEF technology provides significant benefits over conventional IEF 
systems. 
– Baroid’s clay-free IEF design and performance remain unduplicated in the industry. 
– Traditional organophilic clay, used for controlling rheological properties, and organophilic lignite, 
used for filtration control, have been replaced with innovative products resulting from advances 
in fatty acid, tall oil and co-polymer chemistry. 
– This change resulted in major improvements in performance and has been adopted in a wide 
range of base fluids

Environmental Considerations with NAF 
In most regions in the world, land and offshore drilling locations are regulated regarding: 
– Disposal of whole mud 
– Drilled cuttings and solids 
– Water used at rigsite 
– Runoff generated by rain 
Furthermore, the makeup of the fluid itself might be restricted due the presence of several components such 
as: 
– Chlorides 
– Base oil 
– Toxic products 
As we extend drilling into new areas, environmental responsibility and sustainability is becoming a critical 
factor in drilling practices. Minimizing environmental impact is now a significant challenge for operators and

Mixing Oil and Water? 
Simply put, by nature water and oil do not mix. 
That being said, with the use of certain chemicals, namely emulsifiers, the 
two can work together in the same fluid.   
The continuous phase (NAP) will emulsify the water (brine) in what is 
described as an invert emulsion. 
Because the water is part of the discontinuous phase, and it has to be 
carried by the NAP, it will react as a solid from a rheological standpoint, 
increasing the viscosity and ECD of the fluid system.

Unit 25 – Non Aqueous Fluids Water Phase Salinity 
Objectives 
 
Explain the concept of water phase salinity and its importance 
 
Measure the water phase salinity of non-aqueous fluid samples 
Overview 
As mentioned in Unit 23, the water phase salinity is a measure of the osmotic dehydration/hydration potential 
of water in a mud or shale, and it’s dictated by the concentration of salts in the drilling fluid. 
An invert emulsion fluid at minimum will contain one type of salt, usually calcium chloride CaCl2, however, as 
different layers of rock are drilled it’s possible that other salts incorporate into the drilling fluid, such as sodium 
chloride (NaCl). 
To calculate the water phase salinity (WPS), it is necessary to know the following: 
– Whole-drilling fluid calcium content  
– Whole-drilling fluid chloride content 
– Retort fraction of water 
Importance of Water Phase Salinity 
 
The salt will try to balance the exchange of water between the fluid and the formation 
 
Required to avoid formation instability due the water transfer to/from the formation 
 
The water phase salinity analysis helps identify if undissolved salt is present the system. It can 
generate an increase in the solids of the fluid 
 
Variations in the water phase salinity can help to identify if we have contaminations with fresh water or 
brine from the formation

Order of Product Additions 
1. Add the base fluid  
2. Next, add the emulsifiers 
3. Add the alkalinity source 
4. Add brine 
5. Next, viscosifier agents 
6. Next, filtration control agents 
7. Wetting agent 
8. Last add, the weighting material 
 
There are a variety of specific mixing procedures for the mixing order of the fluid systems.  
Please check the design of service for the mixing process of a specific system being built/diluted in the field.
